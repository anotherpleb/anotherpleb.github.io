Investing, explained for complete beginners

The basics in plain English: stocks, bonds, inflation, the Fed, funds, options, and how interest compounds over time. Each section has a simulator you can try, and the last section looks at real people who made or lost money during recent market panics.

Educational only. Not financial advice. Simulators use simple math and assumptions you can edit; real markets are messier. US-centric. The final tab covers the math for anyone curious. The core ideas are summarized in my own words from Burton Malkiel's A Random Walk Down Wall Street (first published 1973; 50th-anniversary 13th edition, 2023). Go read the book.

1. Start here

Investing means giving your money a job. You buy something, like a slice of a company or a loan to a government, because you expect it to pay you back more than you put in. Money sitting in a drawer has no job, and inflation (section 3) slowly eats it.

The one trade-off that explains everything: risk vs. return

Nobody pays you extra for taking no risk. The investments with higher long-run returns are the ones whose value bounces around more along the way. That bouncing is the "price" you pay for the higher return.

Where the money goesHow bumpyRough long-run return
Savings account / Treasury bills (cash)Very smoothLowest, often just around inflation
Bonds (loans to governments and companies)Some bumpsMiddle
Stocks (owning pieces of companies)Big bumps, sometimes −30% or worse in a yearHighest over long periods. The US market has averaged roughly 10% a year before inflation over the past century

These rankings hold over decades, not any given year. The figure for stocks is a widely cited historical average, not a promise.

The big idea from the book: prices take a "random walk"

Malkiel's central argument is that short-term stock moves are close to unpredictable. Prices react to news, and news is surprising by definition. Once something is known, millions of traders have already priced it in. So the next move depends on the next surprise, which nobody can forecast reliably.

This has a practical consequence. If professionals can't reliably predict prices, then paying them high fees to pick stocks or time the market is usually a losing deal. The book famously suggests that a blindfolded monkey throwing darts at the stock listings would pick a portfolio about as good as the experts'. Its alternative: buy the whole market cheaply through an index fund (section 7) and hold it for a long time.

The book tells of a classroom experiment where students built "stock charts" from coin flips, and an expert chart-reader saw real trading signals in them. Try it yourself below.

Coin-flip stock chart

Each trading day the "stock" moves up or down by a random amount. Nothing real is driving it. See how often you spot "trends," "support levels," or "breakouts" anyway.

2. The power of interest

Simple interest pays you only on the money you put in. Compound interest also pays you on the interest you've already earned, so your earnings start earning too. Early on the difference is tiny. After a few decades, the interest-on-interest becomes most of the pile.

The Rule of 72

A quick mental shortcut: divide 72 by the yearly return to get roughly how many years it takes to double your money.

Compounding works against you too. At a 22% credit card rate, an unpaid balance roughly doubles in about 3½ years. That is the same math in reverse.

3. Inflation

Inflation is prices rising over time, so each dollar buys a little less every year. It's usually measured by the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which tracks the cost of a typical basket of goods and services. The Federal Reserve aims for about 2% a year (section 6).

Inflation typically comes from too much money chasing too few goods: strong demand, supply shocks (energy, shipping), or both. In the 2021–2023 surge, US CPI inflation peaked at about 9.1% year over year in June 2022.

What happens to cash under the mattress?
What your cash can buy, in today's dollars

Nominal vs. real returns

The nominal return is the number on your statement. The real return is what's left after inflation, meaning how much more stuff you can actually buy. A savings account paying 4% during 3% inflation only grows your buying power by about 1%.

Real return calculator

Exact formula: (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. "Nominal minus inflation" is the quick approximation.

Takeaway: "Safe" cash isn't risk-free. Its risk is slow and invisible: losing buying power. That's why long-term money usually needs at least some stocks.

4. Stocks

A share of stock is a tiny slice of ownership in a company. If a company has 1 billion shares and you own 100, you own one ten-millionth of it, including that slice of its future profits.

Where stock returns come from

SourceWhat it means
DividendsCash the company pays shareholders out of profits.
Earnings growthThe company gets bigger and more profitable, so each slice is worth more.
Change in mood (valuation)What investors are willing to pay for each dollar of profit, called the price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio. This is the jumpy part. It can double in a boom and halve in a panic.

The first two are driven by the business. The third is driven by people. Malkiel describes two ways of thinking about prices:

  • "Firm foundation" theory: a stock has a real underlying value based on the cash it will produce in the future. Prices eventually return to it.
  • "Castle in the air" theory: a stock is worth whatever the next buyer will pay. Crowd psychology drives prices, and bubbles happen when everyone expects someone else to pay more.

Real markets show both. Over years, profits matter. Over days and weeks, mood swings dominate (see the GameStop and NVIDIA cases in section 9).

Stock indexes

An index is a scorecard for a group of stocks. The S&P 500 tracks about 500 large US companies, weighted by size (market value). Bigger companies count for more. By late 2026, NVIDIA alone made up more than 6% of the index, so drama at one company moved millions of "diversified" portfolios.

Why diversify? One stock vs. many

Every stock carries two kinds of risk. Company risk is a bad CEO, a failed product, or a scandal. Market risk is a recession or a crash that hits everything at once. Owning many companies cancels out most of the company risk, because one firm's disaster is offset by another's success. Nothing cancels market risk.

5. Bonds

A bond is a loan you make to a government or company. In return they promise to pay you interest (the coupon) every year and return the original amount (the face value, often $1,000) on a set date (maturity).

IssuerExampleRisk
US TreasuryTreasury bills, notes, bondsLowest default risk; backed by the US government
Cities and statesMunicipal bondsLow to moderate; interest often tax-advantaged
CompaniesCorporate bondsDepends on the company's credit rating. Low-rated "junk" bonds pay more because they default more often

The rule that confuses everyone: when rates go up, bond prices go down

Say you own a bond paying 3%, and new bonds start paying 5%. Nobody will pay full price for your 3% bond when they can get 5% elsewhere. To sell it, you must cut the price until the buyer's overall return also works out to 5%. The reverse is true too: when rates fall, your older higher-paying bond becomes worth more.

If you hold the bond to maturity (and the issuer doesn't default), you still get every coupon and your full face value back. The price swings only matter if you need to sell early. That turned out to be a fatal problem for a bank in 2023 (section 9).

6. The Fed

The Federal Reserve ("the Fed") is the United States' central bank. Congress gave it two main goals, called the dual mandate: maximum employment and stable prices. It defines stable prices as about 2% inflation a year.

Its main tools

ToolPlain English
The federal funds rateThe Fed sets a target for the overnight rate banks charge each other. It ripples out into mortgages, car loans, credit cards, and savings accounts.
Buying or selling bondsBuying bonds ("quantitative easing") pushes long-term rates down and adds money to the system. Shrinking its holdings ("quantitative tightening") does the opposite.
Emergency lenderIn a panic, the Fed can lend to banks so a bank run doesn't spread. This happened in March 2023 after Silicon Valley Bank failed.

How a rate change ripples through everything

These are the usual directions, not guarantees. Markets often move ahead of the Fed, reacting to what they expect it to do rather than what it just did.

Recent example: To fight the 2021–2022 inflation surge, the Fed raised its rate from near 0% in early 2022 to above 5% by mid-2023, its fastest hiking campaign since the early 1980s. Bond prices fell sharply in 2022, savings accounts finally paid real interest again, and one bank that had loaded up on low-rate bonds collapsed (section 9).

For the current rate and the Fed's latest decisions, check federalreserve.gov. This page deliberately doesn't hard-code a number that will go stale.

7. Mutual funds vs. index funds

Buying hundreds of stocks one at a time is impractical. A fund pools money from many investors and buys a basket for all of them. You own a slice of the basket.

Actively managed mutual fundIndex fund
StrategyA manager tries to pick winners and beat the marketBuys everything in an index (like the S&P 500) and holds it
Typical yearly fee (expense ratio)Often around 0.5%–1%+Broad index funds can be well under 0.1%
GoalBeat the indexMatch the index, minus a tiny fee

ETF vs. mutual fund: an ETF (exchange-traded fund) is a fund that trades on the stock exchange throughout the day like a stock. "Index" vs. "active" describes the strategy; "ETF" vs. "mutual fund" describes the wrapper. You can have index mutual funds and active ETFs.

The book's core argument, and the data behind it

If prices take a random walk, most professional stock-pickers can't consistently beat the market, especially after their fees. Malkiel made this argument in 1973, before index funds were even available to regular investors. The data has backed him up since:

  • In 2025, 79% of actively managed US large-company stock funds trailed the S&P 500 (S&P Dow Jones Indices' SPIVA scorecard).
  • Over the 15 years through 2024, about 90% trailed it.
  • Last year's winners rarely stay winners. That makes it hard to pick the few that will beat the index in advance.

Fees are the one thing you can control. Across all equity mutual funds offered in 2025, the simple average expense ratio was about 1.09% (Investment Company Institute). Investors who choose carefully pay far less.

The catch with "owning the whole market": the S&P 500 is weighted by size, so a handful of giant companies drive a big share of its moves. You're diversified, but not equally across companies. See the NVIDIA case in section 9.

8. Calls & puts

Advanced and high-risk. Options are not needed for ordinary long-term investing. They're here so the headlines make sense. Many beginners who trade short-term options lose most of what they put in.

An option is a contract that gives you the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell a stock at a set price (the strike) before a set date (expiration). You pay up front for that right; the price is the premium. In the US, one contract usually covers 100 shares.

You profit if the stock…Most you can lose (as the buyer)Everyday analogy
Call = right to buyrises well above the strikeThe premium you paidA non-refundable deposit that locks in a house price
Put = right to sellfalls well below the strikeThe premium you paidInsurance on your car: it pays out when something bad happens

Every option has a seller on the other side who collects the premium and takes on the obligation. Selling a call on a stock you don't own (a "naked call") has unlimited possible losses, because there's no ceiling on how high a stock can go.

Option payoff at expiration
Option profit / lossSame cash spent on shares instead

Profit at the expiration date only, ignoring commissions. Before expiration an option's price also depends on time remaining and expected volatility. Real option pricing is more complicated.

Leverage: why options make headlines

In the default example, $400 buys one call controlling 100 shares, or just 4 shares outright. If the stock jumps 20% to $120, the call earns $1,100 (+275%) while the 4 shares earn $80 (+20%). If the stock just sits at $100, the call expires worthless (−100%) while the shares lose nothing. Options magnify both directions, and time works against the buyer.

Short selling (related, not an option)

Shorting means borrowing shares, selling them, and hoping to buy them back cheaper later. Your gain is capped at 100% (the stock can only fall to zero), but your possible loss is unlimited. When a heavily shorted stock rises fast, short sellers rush to buy back shares, pushing the price even higher. That's a short squeeze (see GameStop in section 9).

9. Real-world drama: who made and lost money

Theory is easy when markets are calm. These are six real episodes from the last few years, using real closing prices. In each one, imaginary investors with $10,000 make the choices real people made. The investors are hypothetical; the prices are real.

Oct–Nov 2026NVIDIA: the CEO scandal and the biggest one-day gain in history

NVIDIA, the world's most valuable chipmaker, hit a record close of $238.90 on October 5, 2026. Then a scandal around its co-founder and CEO, Jensen Huang, combined with existing worries about an AI bubble, sent the stock on one of the wildest rides any giant company has had.

  • Oct 5: Record close of $238.90.
  • Oct 29: The New York Times reports Huang had a close relationship with a woman US officials suspect of being a Chinese intelligence asset.
  • Nov 8–9: Huang admits an affair; the board opens an independent investigation. The stock falls 4.3% on Monday, erasing about $192 billion in value.
  • Nov 11: Closes at $173.73, then drops about 8% after hours on a report of a second relationship, with a direct report.
  • Nov 12: Huang resigns; CFO Colette Kress becomes interim CEO. The stock opens at $151, then recovers to close at $166.19 (−4.3%).
  • Nov 20: Reuters reports an SEC probe into NVIDIA's accounting. The stock falls 14% to $125.53, about 47% below the October record. The Nasdaq ends the week down 10%, its worst week since early 2020.
  • Nov 23: The board reinstates Huang as CEO. The stock jumps 31.5% to $165.05, which MarketWatch called the biggest one-day gain (in market value) in stock-market history.

10. The math (for the curious)

Everything on this page runs on a handful of formulas. Here they are, with the intuition behind each and worked examples taken from the earlier sections. Nothing beyond algebra and logarithms, plus one formula with a bell curve at the end.

Notation: r = yearly rate as a decimal (7% → 0.07), t = years, n = compounding periods per year, ln = natural log, σ (sigma) = volatility, the standard deviation of returns.

1. Compound growth

FV = P × (1 + r/n)n·t

Each period multiplies the balance by (1 + r/n). After n·t periods, you've multiplied that many times. Compound more often and, in the limit, you get continuous compounding:

FV = P × er·t

The gain from compounding more often is small. $1,000 at 7% for 40 years becomes $14,974 compounded yearly, $16,326 monthly, and $16,445 continuously. Rate and time matter far more than frequency.

Regular contributions form a geometric series. A deposit C made each period grows for a different number of periods, and summing them gives:

FV = C × [ (1 + i)N − 1 ] ÷ i

Here i is the rate per deposit period and N is the number of deposits. The simulator in section 2 uses an effective monthly rate, i = (1 + r/n)n/12 − 1, so monthly deposits work with any compounding choice.

Early Emma, checked by hand: $200/month at 7% compounded monthly (i = 0.07/12 ≈ 0.005833) for 120 months gives 200 × (1.005833120 − 1) ÷ 0.005833 ≈ $34,600 at age 35. Then 30 years of growth with no deposits: × 1.005833360 ≈ × 8.12 → ≈ $281,000. Larry's 360 deposits: 200 × (8.12 − 1) ÷ 0.005833 ≈ $244,000.

2. Why the Rule of 72 works

Doubling means (1 + r)t = 2. Take logs of both sides:

t = ln 2 ÷ ln(1 + r) ≈ 0.693 ÷ r × (1 + r/2)

The approximation uses ln(1 + r) ≈ r − r²/2. At typical rates around 8%, the correction factor (1 + 0.04) turns 69.3 into about 72. The number 72 also divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, and 12, which makes mental math easy. With continuous compounding, the exact rule is the "Rule of 69.3."

3. Real returns (the Fisher equation)

1 + real = (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation)

The shortcut "nominal − inflation" overstates the real return by roughly real × inflation. That error is tiny at 2–3% inflation and matters at 1970s-style rates.

4. Averages lie: arithmetic vs. geometric returns

Lose 50%, then gain 50%. The arithmetic average return is 0%, but you're down 25%, because 0.5 × 1.5 = 0.75. What you actually earn is the geometric average, and volatility always drags it below the arithmetic average:

geometric ≈ arithmetic − σ² ÷ 2

This "volatility drag" is why a steady 10% beats a bumpy average of 10%. It's also why 2× and inverse ETFs (like the 2× short NVIDIA fund in the case study) slowly lose value in choppy markets even when the stock goes nowhere. They reset every day, so the drag compounds.

Steady: same return every year (the arithmetic average)Bumpy: alternates good and bad years

5. Recovery math: why losses hurt more than gains help

After a loss L, you need a gain of:

required gain = L ÷ (1 − L)

6. Volatility grows with the square root of time

In a random walk, daily moves are independent, so their variances add rather than their standard deviations. Over t days, the spread grows with √t, not t:

σannual ≈ σdaily × √252   (about 252 trading days per year)

A stock that typically moves 1% a day has roughly 16% annual volatility. The coin-flip chart in section 1 moves 0.5–2% a day (a typical daily move of about 1.3%), which works out to about 21% a year, in the range of a fairly volatile large stock. The same math explains why a year's return is a coin toss but a 30-year return is much more predictable: the expected gain grows with t while the noise only grows with √t.

7. Diversification as a formula

The simulator in section 4 uses a simple "single-index" model. Each stock's return is the market return plus its own independent noise. For an equal-weight portfolio of N stocks:

σportfolio² = σmarket² + σcompany² ÷ N

With the simulator's numbers (16% market, 30% company): one stock gives √(0.16² + 0.30²) ≈ 34%; 100 stocks give √(0.16² + 0.30²/100) ≈ 16.3%. The company term shrinks toward zero, but the market term never does. That's "you can diversify away company risk but not market risk," in one line.

For any two assets with weights w₁, w₂ and correlation ρ (rho, from −1 to +1):

σp² = w₁²σ₁² + w₂²σ₂² + 2·w₁w₂·ρ·σ₁σ₂

The lower the correlation, the more the third term cancels out. That's why stocks and high-quality bonds are often paired. In a crisis, correlations among stocks tend to jump toward 1, which is why diversification feels weakest exactly when you want it most.

8. Bond pricing, duration, and convexity

A bond's price is the present value of every payment it will make, discounted at today's market rate y:

Price = Σ C ÷ (1 + y)t  +  Face ÷ (1 + y)T

Raise y and every denominator grows, so the price falls. That's the inverse rule in one formula. Macaulay duration is the present-value-weighted average time until you get paid. Modified duration D* = D ÷ (1 + y) converts it into price sensitivity:

ΔP ÷ P ≈ −D* × Δy  +  ½ × Convexity × (Δy)²

The SVB-style example from section 5 (1.8% coupon, 4 years left, market rate jumps from 1.8% to 4.75%, so Δy = 0.0295):

  • Modified duration ≈ 3.83, so the duration-only estimate is −3.83 × 0.0295 ≈ −11.3%.
  • Convexity ≈ 18.6 adds back ½ × 18.6 × 0.0295² ≈ +0.8%, giving −10.5%.
  • The exact repricing is $894.78, or −10.5%. The two-term approximation is almost perfect.

Convexity is why the price curve in the bond simulator bends: price losses slow down as rates rise, and gains speed up as rates fall.

9. Fee drag, quantified

A fee f turns each year's growth factor from (1 + r) into (1 + r − f). Over t years, a lump sum ends up at about:

(1 + r − f)t ÷ (1 + r)t ≈ e−f·t of what it would have been with no fee

A 0.95-point fee gap over 30 years gives e−0.285 ≈ 0.75, so a lump sum ends up about 25% smaller. The fee simulator in section 7 shows a smaller hit (about 18%) because monthly contributions made in later years pay the fee for fewer years.

10. What is a stock "worth"? The Gordon growth model

The "firm foundation" view in its simplest form: if a stock pays a dividend D next year that grows at rate g forever, and investors demand return r, then:

Price = D ÷ (r − g)
Why small changes in assumptions swing prices wildly

Because r − g is a small number in the denominator, nudging growth from 5% to 6% raises the "fair" price by 50%, and raising the required return from 8% to 9% cuts it by 25%. That sensitivity is why growth stocks swing so violently on news about their future (AI demand, a CEO's exit) and on interest rates (section 6).

11. Options: payoffs, parity, and Black–Scholes

At expiration, payoffs are simple:

Call = max(S − K, 0)     Put = max(K − S, 0)

Put–call parity ties the prices of a call and a put with the same strike and expiration together. If it didn't hold, traders could make riskless money:

C − P = S − K·e−rT

Before expiration, the classic Black–Scholes model prices a European call by assuming the stock follows a random walk with constant volatility (log-normal returns):

C = S·N(d₁) − K·e−rT·N(d₂)
d₁ = [ ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2)·T ] ÷ (σ√T),    d₂ = d₁ − σ√T

N(·) is the standard normal cumulative distribution (the area under the bell curve). N(d₁) is the option's delta: roughly how many dollars the option moves per $1 move in the stock.

Black–Scholes calculator

The defaults match the option example in section 8. At 40% volatility and 30 days, the fair call price is about $2.71, less than the $4 premium assumed there. Raise volatility and watch both options get more expensive: uncertainty is what option buyers pay for.

Where the model breaks: Black–Scholes assumes smooth, continuous price changes with constant volatility. Real markets jump. NVIDIA's −14% and +31.5% days in November 2026, two trading days apart, are exactly the kind of moves the model treats as nearly impossible. Real option prices reflect that with higher volatility for far-from-the-money strikes (the "volatility smile").
